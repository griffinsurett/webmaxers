  <section>
    <h2>How fast should a website load?</h2>
    <p>
      The main page content should be visible within <strong>2.5 seconds</strong>, the page should respond to a tap or click within <strong>200 milliseconds</strong>, and the layout should barely move while it loads. Those are Google's "good" thresholds for its Core Web Vitals, and they are the clearest targets a business site can aim for.
    </p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Main content visible (Largest Contentful Paint):</strong> 2.5 seconds or less is good. Over 4 seconds is poor.</li>
      <li><strong>Responds to input (Interaction to Next Paint):</strong> 200 milliseconds or less is good. Over 500 milliseconds is poor.</li>
      <li><strong>Layout stays put (Cumulative Layout Shift):</strong> 0.1 or less is good. Over 0.25 is poor.</li>
    </ul>
    <p>
      Google measures these on real visits, and a page passes when at least 75% of those visits meet the "good" threshold. That makes them a test of what most of your visitors actually experience, not just the best case on a fast office connection.
    </p>
  </section>

  <section>
    <h2>What is a good PageSpeed score?</h2>
    <p>
      In Google PageSpeed Insights, a performance score of <strong>90–100 is good</strong>, 50–89 needs improvement, and 0–49 is poor. The mobile score is the one to watch: it simulates a mid-range phone on a slower connection, so it is almost always lower than desktop, and it is closer to how many of your visitors browse.
    </p>
    <p>
      Treat the score as a diagnostic, not the goal. It comes from a single simulated lab test, and it can vary between runs. The Core Web Vitals section at the top of the report, labelled "Discover what your real users are experiencing", is based on real visitors. When the two disagree, that real-user data is what reflects your visitors' experience.
    </p>
  </section>

  <section>
    <h2>Core Web Vitals in plain English</h2>
    <p>
      Core Web Vitals are three measurements Google uses to judge whether a page feels fast and stable to use. Each one maps to a question a visitor would ask without thinking about it.
    </p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Largest Contentful Paint (LCP), "Is it loading?":</strong> how long it takes for the biggest thing on screen, usually the hero image or headline, to appear.</li>
      <li><strong>Interaction to Next Paint (INP), "Is it responding?":</strong> how quickly the page reacts when someone taps a button, opens a menu or types in a form.</li>
      <li><strong>Cumulative Layout Shift (CLS), "Is it stable?":</strong> how much the page jumps around while it loads, like a button moving just as you go to tap it.</li>
    </ul>
    <p>
      A page can score well on one and badly on another. A site can show its headline quickly and still feel broken if every tap lags, or if the content keeps shifting as ads and images load in. For a closer look at each score and how to check yours, see <a href="/blog/core-web-vitals">Core Web Vitals explained</a>.
    </p>
  </section>

  <section>
    <h2>Speed changes the first impression before your copy even has a chance</h2>
    <p>
      When a website loads slowly, people start forming negative conclusions before they read a headline or look at a service. The business can feel outdated, disorganized, or less trustworthy even if the offer itself is strong.
    </p>
    <p>
      Fast websites feel more credible. Pages respond quickly, the layout settles into place without awkward jumps, and visitors can move through the site without friction. That smoother first impression matters because most businesses only get a short window to prove they are worth paying attention to.
    </p>
  </section>

  <section>
    <h2>Slow websites quietly reduce conversions</h2>
    <p>
      A slow site does not always fail in obvious ways. More often, it leaks performance one impatient visitor at a time. People bounce before they reach the call to action, abandon forms partway through, or never wait long enough to see the proof that would have convinced them.
    </p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Lead generation suffers:</strong> Contact forms, quote flows, and booking actions lose momentum when each page feels sluggish.</li>
      <li><strong>Calls to action get ignored:</strong> Visitors are less likely to keep scrolling when the site already feels slow or unstable.</li>
      <li><strong>Paid traffic becomes less efficient:</strong> You can spend to get a click, but speed problems can waste that click before the landing page has done its job.</li>
    </ul>
  </section>

  <section>
    <h2>Mobile visitors feel speed problems first</h2>
    <p>
      Many business owners review their site on a strong laptop connection and assume the experience is fine. Real visitors are often on phones, weaker connections, and interrupted attention spans. That is where speed issues become expensive.
    </p>
    <p>
      Heavy images, bloated scripts, layout shifts, autoplaying distractions, and poorly handled third-party tools all hit mobile users harder. If the site is supposed to help busy people take action quickly, performance has to be treated like part of usability, not a technical side quest.
    </p>
  </section>

  <section>
    <h2>How website speed affects SEO</h2>
    <p>
      Core Web Vitals are part of the page experience signals Google uses in ranking. They are not a shortcut to the top: relevant, useful content still matters most. But when several pages answer a search equally well, the faster, more stable one has the edge.
    </p>
    <p>
      Speed also helps indirectly. Visitors who do not bounce straight back to the results are more likely to read, click through and convert, and a lean site is easier for search engines to crawl. Speed alone will not rank a bad page, but poor performance can absolutely hold back a good one.
    </p>
  </section>

  <section>
    <h2>7 fixes that make the biggest difference</h2>
    <p>
      Most slow business sites are slow for the same handful of reasons. These are the fixes that usually move the numbers most, roughly in order of impact.
    </p>
    <ol>
      <li><strong>Resize and compress images.</strong> Serve modern formats like WebP or AVIF at the size they are actually displayed. Oversized photos are the most common cause of a slow Largest Contentful Paint.</li>
      <li><strong>Load the hero first and everything else later.</strong> Prioritize the image or headline at the top of the page, and lazy-load images and video further down so they do not compete with it.</li>
      <li><strong>Cut third-party scripts.</strong> Chat widgets, trackers, pop-ups and embedded feeds each add weight and delay. Remove what you do not use and load the rest after the page is usable.</li>
      <li><strong>Ship less JavaScript.</strong> Heavy themes, page builders and stacks of plugins send code every visitor has to download and run, which is what makes taps feel laggy.</li>
      <li><strong>Reserve space for images, embeds and ads.</strong> Giving every element its dimensions up front stops the page jumping as it loads, which fixes layout shift directly.</li>
      <li><strong>Use fast hosting and a CDN.</strong> A content delivery network serves your pages from a location near each visitor, and good hosting gets the first byte to them quickly.</li>
      <li><strong>Handle fonts carefully.</strong> Use fewer font files, host them yourself where you can, and let text show in a fallback font while the custom one loads.</li>
    </ol>
    <p>
      The best time to apply all of this is during design and development. It is much easier to choose lighter patterns and smarter integrations early than to clean up a bloated system after launch.
    </p>
  </section>
